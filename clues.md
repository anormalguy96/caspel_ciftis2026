Absolutely. Here’s the **full 30-point “vibe-coded website” list with clear definitions**, not just the names. These are best understood as **visual/copy patterns that often appear together in AI-generated SaaS sites**—not proof by themselves that AI built the site.

1. **Harsh gradients** — Strong, high-contrast gradients used across hero sections, buttons, headings, or backgrounds, often with purple/blue/pink transitions. They make the design feel flashy quickly, but when overused they can look template-generated rather than brand-specific.

2. **Lucide icons** — Heavy reliance on the Lucide icon library for nearly every visual cue. Lucide itself is excellent, but AI-generated React interfaces frequently default to it, so an entire product using nothing but familiar Lucide symbols can feel generic.

3. **Pure white background** — Large sections using completely white backgrounds with little texture, layering, visual hierarchy, or brand character. White is not a problem; the issue is when the whole site feels like an untouched default canvas.

4. **Rainbow coloring** — Multiple unrelated accent colors used simultaneously across icons, cards, badges, headings, and illustrations. It often appears when the design tries to communicate “modern,” “creative,” or “AI-powered” without a disciplined color system.

5. **Drop shadows** — Frequent shadows around cards, buttons, modals, and panels to create instant depth. Shadows are normal UI design, but excessive or inconsistent shadows can make a site resemble generic component-library output.

6. **Three feature cards in a row** — The classic section containing exactly three horizontally aligned cards, each with an icon, title, and short paragraph. This is one of the most common SaaS and AI-generated landing-page patterns because it is easy to produce and visually balanced.

7. **Emojis** — Emojis used as section icons, feature illustrations, category markers, or decorative elements instead of a deliberately designed iconography or illustration system. They can make the UI feel improvised if they do not match the product identity.

9. **Em dashes in marketing copy** — Frequent use of “—” in headlines and paragraphs, especially in polished marketing sentences. This is less a visual issue and more a copywriting pattern often associated with AI-generated prose.

10. **Inter font everywhere** — Using extremely common modern tech fonts without customization or a broader typography system. These fonts are good, but they have become defaults across generated SaaS interfaces, making many sites visually indistinguishable.

11. **Colored left stripe** — Cards, alerts, testimonials, or information boxes with a thin colored vertical border on the left. It is a convenient way to add hierarchy, but it is frequently produced as a default component treatment.

12. **Fake testimonials** — Testimonials from invented users, fake companies, generated avatars, or unverifiable customers presented as social proof. Unlike most items here, this is not merely an aesthetic cliché; if represented as genuine, it is deceptive.

13. **Bento grids** — Dashboard-like layouts made from differently sized rounded cards arranged in a modular grid. Bento design can be effective, but it became heavily overused in AI startup sites because it makes almost any product look polished without requiring a unique information architecture.

14. **Terminal window mockups** — Decorative fake command-line interfaces showing commands, code, installation instructions, or animated typing. They are especially common on developer-tool and AI-product landing pages, even when the terminal is not central to the actual product.

15. **“It’s not X, it’s Y” copy** — Marketing sentences such as “It’s not just a tool—it’s your AI copilot” or “Not another CRM. Your growth engine.” This contrast formula is extremely common in AI-generated marketing copy and often feels generic.

16. **Checkmark bullets** — Feature lists where every benefit begins with a green check icon. This is functional and common, especially in pricing pages, but repeated everywhere it can make the site feel like an automatically generated SaaS template.

17. **Three pricing tiers** — The predictable “Starter / Pro / Enterprise” or “Free / Pro / Business” three-column pricing layout, usually with the center plan visually highlighted. This is a legitimate SaaS convention, but AI-generated sites often produce it whether the business model actually requires it or not.

18. **No real product demos** — The landing page talks extensively about features but shows few or no genuine screenshots, interactions, workflows, videos, or realistic product states. This is a stronger signal because generated sites often focus on marketing structure before demonstrating an actual product.

19. **Soft corner radius everywhere** — Almost every card, button, image, modal, input, and container uses medium-to-large rounded corners. Rounded corners are normal today, but uniform softness across everything can make the design feel like a default component system instead of an intentional brand language.

20. **Purple + black color scheme** — Dark backgrounds paired with purple, violet, indigo, or neon-blue highlights. This became the stereotypical visual language of AI startups, crypto products, and developer tools, so using it without differentiation can immediately create an “AI template” impression.

21. **No skeleton loaders** — Dynamic screens jump from empty states directly to fully loaded content, rather than using skeleton placeholders or thoughtfully designed loading states. This is not primarily an aesthetic cliché; it can indicate that the UI was assembled quickly without fully considering production interaction states.

22. **Radial glowing orbs** — Large blurry circles or gradient glows positioned behind hero text, cards, dashboards, or CTAs. They are commonly used to make otherwise empty layouts feel visually rich and are one of the most recognizable AI-startup design clichés.

23. **Dot grids** — Decorative backgrounds made of small repeated dots, especially behind hero sections, diagrams, or developer-tool content. They imply “technical infrastructure” or “network systems” visually, but are heavily reused across generated tech sites.

24. **Sparkle icons ✨** — Stars, magic-wand symbols, sparkles, or four-point star icons used to represent AI functionality. Because “sparkle = AI” has become nearly universal, heavy use of these icons makes the design immediately resemble generic AI-product branding.

25. **Animated arrows** — Moving arrows, curved lines, bouncing indicators, or animated connectors showing that users should scroll, click, or follow a workflow. These can aid comprehension, but AI-built sites frequently add them as decorative motion rather than because the interaction actually requires them.

26. **No Terms of Service** — A production-facing service has no Terms page, legal agreement, or accessible link to terms. This is not a visual vibe-code signal itself; it more strongly suggests the product is unfinished, prototyped, or launched without operational/legal completeness.

27. **No Privacy Policy** — The website collects accounts, analytics, messages, uploads, contact information, or other user data but has no privacy policy. Again, this indicates incomplete productization rather than a specific visual style and can become a serious compliance issue.

28. **Hover animations everywhere** — Cards lift, scale, glow, rotate, shift, or change borders whenever the pointer moves over them. Some hover feedback is good UX; excessive animation often signals that visual excitement was prioritized over interaction purpose.

29. **Neon colors** — Bright electric purple, cyan, green, magenta, or blue accents used against dark surfaces. Neon palettes are strongly associated with “future/AI/tech” aesthetics and can look generic when they are not connected to a distinctive brand identity.

30. **Basic pastel colors** — Soft lavender, mint, peach, baby blue, pink, or cream used across cards and illustrations in a very safe, pleasant way. Pastels are not inherently AI-like, but generic pastel palettes are often chosen by generated designs because they create quick visual friendliness without requiring a deeper brand system.

The important pattern is that **none of these individually means “vibe-coded.”** A site starts strongly giving that impression when many of them appear together with generic copy, generic component structure, weak product-specific visuals, and little evidence of a deliberate design system.
